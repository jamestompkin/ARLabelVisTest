# Critical assessment — farthest-point label colour selection

Written 2026-04-25. Companion to `NIGHTLY_LOG_2026-04-25.md`.

This is a red-team review of the *idea*, not the code. It exists to
sharpen what the IEEE VIS short paper claims and what reviewers will
push back on. Treat it as a checklist for the discussion section and
the rebuttal, not as a verdict.

## tl;dr

The objective is sensible and the candidate-set machinery is genuinely
novel. The *evaluation* is asking the reader to accept three things on
faith:

1. that single-pixel maximum-perceptual-distance is the right target,
2. that "perceptual distance" is a good proxy for legibility, and
3. that the pipeline's picks are temporally stable in an AR setting.

A reviewer will press on (2) and (3) hardest. Pick one and address it
quantitatively before submission.

---

## Is this gamut mapping?

**No.** Gamut mapping minimises ‖input − output‖ subject to the output
being inside a target gamut — its semantic is "preserve the input as
faithfully as possible on this monitor." Yours maximises ‖input −
output‖ — the semantic is "output the colour least like the input, so
a label drawn over it stands out."

Same machinery (perceptual space, gamut-shaped candidate set,
256³-LUT bake-out), opposite optimisation. Closer relatives:
maximally-distinct-colour-palette work like Glasbey 2007, Heer–Stone
2012, Wijffelaars 2008, where a fixed K colours are chosen to be far
apart. The twist here is that K is implicit in a candidate gamut/mesh
and the *input pixel* indexes which farthest-colour the LUT returns.

The candidate-set construction (`bind_lab_to_sphere`, gaussian-
smoothed hull, neural inner-bound) **is** an inward projection of the
gamut surface — that operation in isolation looks like gamut
compression. But you're not displaying those projected colours;
you're using them as the *search domain* the farthest-point operator
picks from. The compression is a regulariser on the search space, not
a perceptual-faithfulness target.

A line in related work distinguishing the two is non-negotiable;
reviewers will absolutely raise this.

## What the idea gets right

### The objective is well-posed for a real problem
AR area labels need to be readable against arbitrary backgrounds.
Picking output = farthest from background in a perceptual metric is
a defensible operationalisation of "maximise contrast." Sampling the
*gamut* as the candidate set guarantees the chosen output is actually
displayable — that alone is a contribution over naive
complement-of-CEC schemes.

### Working in a perceptual space is correct
sRGB+Euclidean trivially picks the 8 cube corners (figure: K_eff=11
from interpolation blends). CIELAB methods get K_eff in the tens to
hundreds. The figure makes this immediate.

### The geodesic-on-mesh framing has merit
RGD on a mesh of the gamut respects the *manifold* of displayable
colours. A straight CIELAB Euclidean distance ignores the gamut's
non-convex pockets; a geodesic-on-the-hull respects them. This is
the genuinely novel contribution — and the bit reviewers will
recognise as the technical core.

### The α̂ regularisation knob has the right *shape*
Tuning between "geodesic" (faithful but spiky) and "smoothed" (less
flicker between adjacent frames) is exactly the trade-off an AR
deployment cares about. The knob exists in the right place. The
specific defaults are a separate question (see below).

## What's underexamined or weak

### "Farthest" is a single-pixel objective in a multi-pixel problem
A label is N pixels wide. The optimal colour against pixel A might
be terrible against pixel B in the same label. The CEC step (27-bin
tertile mode) collapses the label region to one representative
colour — but that's a heuristic. The optimisation is over a single
CEC, not over the actual label region's colour distribution.

A more rigorous formulation would be:

```
argmax_c min_{p ∈ label} d(c, scene(p))     (maximin)
```

or its expectation form under the pixel histogram. The current
method approximates the former via mode-of-tertile and the latter
not at all.

**What the paper needs:** justify why CEC is a good summary of the
label region (e.g. show that maximin and CEC pick the same colour
on most labels) or admit it's a coarse approximation in the
limitations.

### Farthest is not the same as legible
Two colours can be perceptually far apart (large ΔE₀₀) and still
produce illegible text. The canonical case is yellow on white:
ΔE₀₀ ≈ 90, WCAG luminance contrast ≈ 1.07, illegible. The
accessibility literature uses WCAG luminance contrast, not
perceptual distance, as the readability axis. ΔE correlates with
luminance contrast in many cases but not always — yellow-on-white,
magenta-on-cyan, light-cyan-on-light-yellow, etc.

**What the paper needs:** a side-by-side histogram of WCAG
luminance contrast for ours vs Kwon's picks across the gamut. If
both methods produce uniformly high contrast, this is fine to
mention and move on. If ours produces some low-contrast picks (the
yellow-on-white pathology) and Kwon doesn't, you have a problem and
need to explain why ΔE is still the right objective. A reviewer
with an accessibility background will press on this hard.

### The candidate-set ablation is what makes the paper, and isn't argued strongly enough
Three claims to defend:

**1. Why bound at all** (instead of using the raw gamut hull?)
   - Implicit answer: "because the hull surface is non-smooth and
     produces unstable picks under input perturbations."
   - That needs to be *shown* (e.g. ‖f(x+ε) − f(x)‖ histogram for
     small ε on the hull vs the smoothed bound), not asserted.

**2. Why an *inner* bound rather than the gamut itself**
   - The inner bound throws away the most-saturated displayable
     colours, which is exactly where farthest-point would pick from.
   - You need to argue temporal stability + smoothness outweighs the
     lost saturation.
   - The α̂=1.25 → K_eff=6 result *argues against* over-
     regularisation; α̂=0.05 with K_eff=28 looks better. The paper
     should either explain that K_eff=6 is fine (which would need
     justification) or pick α̂=0.05 as the headline and explain why
     more regularisation hurts.

**3. Why neural rather than a closed-form bound** (sphere, gaussian-
   smoothed hull)?
   - Sphere has K_eff=954, way more than neural's 28. So is sphere
     better?
   - Probably the sphere palette is dominated by *low-chroma centre*
     colours (visible in the palette panel of `palette_geometry.png`
     — the sphere row's output palette is a tight blob in the middle,
     hardly any high-chroma entries). Many of those 954 entries are
     pastel.
   - That argument needs to be quantitative: e.g. "ΔE₀₀ to white"
     histogram of the palette entries shows sphere clusters near
     white whereas neural spreads.

### There's no temporal evaluation
AR is dynamic. Frames change, CEC drifts, the picked colour can hop
between palette entries frame to frame. That hopping — *not* per-
frame quality — is the real failure mode of an AR label. None of
the current figures show temporal behaviour. `tab_scene` gestures
at it with per-frame RGB gradients, but the paper needs:

```
flicker_energy(α̂) = Σ_t ‖ f(CEC_t) − f(CEC_{t-1}) ‖²
```

plotted as a curve in α̂. *That* is the chart that justifies RGD
over Euclidean, beyond aesthetic claims about smoothness. Without
it, the α̂ knob's whole reason for existing isn't measured.

### The regularised geodesic is taken on faith
Edelstein 2023 introduced RGD for nearest-point queries, where a
smoother distance field gives stabler retrieval. For *farthest-
point* it's not obvious smoothing helps — the maximum of a smoothed
function can move farther from the maximum of the underlying
function as α̂ grows. Your α̂=1.25 → K_eff=6 collapse is suspicious
and consistent with that concern.

The paper should either:
- show empirically that the farthest-point operator is well-behaved
  under RGD smoothing (e.g. a Lipschitz-like bound on how the
  argmax moves as α̂ increases), or
- acknowledge that α̂ has a sweet spot and the figure exists *to
  find it*.

### The pipeline has too many knobs to defend
`LutConfig` has 11 axes: input_space, working_space, output_space,
shape, smoothing, shape_gaussian_sigma, shape_neural_bound_bias,
shape_neural_iterations, metric, metric_rgd_alpha_hat, interval,
voxel_dim. A reviewer will ask "why these defaults?" and the answer
needs to be either "we ablated them" (which the figures don't do
exhaustively) or "they don't matter much" (which the figures don't
show).

Concrete suggestion: half the axes could be defaulted internally
and never surfaced in the paper. The user-facing story should be
something like:

```
working_space ∈ {CIELAB, OKLAB}    # which perceptual space
candidate_set ∈ {hull, smoothed, neural}    # which gamut bound
metric ∈ {Euclidean, ΔE00, RGD}    # which distance
α̂ ∈ R+    # RGD smoothing strength
```

Four knobs. Defend those four.

### The 256³-LUT framing leaks an architectural assumption
Baking f: sRGB → sRGB into a static lookup table is correct for
an AR shader, but it forecloses *any* input-aware adaptation:
the function is by construction a pixel-wise map, not a region-
aware one. If a reviewer says "the *neighbourhood* of a pixel is
what makes a label readable, not the pixel itself," your answer
has to be: "we capture neighbourhood through the upstream CEC
step; the LUT is the pure colour-pair operation."

That's a defensible factoring, but it should be explicit in the
method section, not implicit.

## What would strengthen the case

In rough priority order:

1. **A maximin (or histogram-expectation) baseline against the label
   region's pixel distribution**, even just on a handful of frames.
   If maximin gives effectively the same pick as CEC+LUT, you've
   justified the simpler pipeline. If not, you've found a real
   improvement to claim or a limitation to scope.

2. **A WCAG-luminance-contrast histogram**, ours vs Kwon. Reframe
   the contrast story so a reviewer can't blindside you with "but
   ΔE isn't legibility." Either confirm ours stays in the legible
   regime, or scope the limitation explicitly.

3. **Temporal-stability metric on at least one video.** Per-frame
   ‖f(CEC_t) − f(CEC_{t-1})‖, plotted vs α̂. This is the curve
   that *justifies* RGD as a method choice — not just as a smooth-
   things-out hyperparameter.

4. **Cut the user-facing knob count.** Move four-five `LutConfig`
   fields into "internal defaults" with a one-line note.

5. **Explicit ablation table:** rows = candidate sets, columns =
   metrics, entries = K_eff and median WCAG contrast. Eight or so
   numbers in one table beats the 9-row palette figure for a
   reviewer in a hurry.

---

## Bottom line

The pipeline does something genuinely new — RGD on a neurally-
bounded gamut for farthest-point colour selection isn't in the
literature. The objective is sensible. The figures show the
machinery works.

The risks for the paper are about the *frame*, not the *method*:

- gamut-mapping confusion → fixable with two sentences in related work
- single-pixel objective → fixable by acknowledging in limitations or
  adding a maximin baseline
- ΔE vs WCAG contrast → fixable with one extra histogram
- no temporal evaluation → fixable with one extra figure
- too many knobs → fixable with editorial cuts

The technical contribution is real. The paper just needs to be more
honest about which trade-offs it's making and which it's choosing not
to evaluate.
